Websites 5 min read

What actually makes a business website convert

It isn't the animation, the font, or the colour of the button. After building sites for tradespeople, salons, dealerships and schools, the same three things decide whether a visitor gets in touch.

I've watched people use the sites I build, and the pattern is boringly consistent. Nobody reads a homepage. They scan it for about four seconds looking for one answer: can this business do the thing I need, and can I reach them?

1. Say what you do in the first line

Not "Welcome to our website." Not "Excellence in service since 2009." Say the thing. "Emergency plumbing in Nelson, 24 hours." "Electric vehicles, in stock, test drive today." A visitor who has to work out what you sell will leave before they finish the sentence.

2. Put the contact route everywhere, not on a contact page

The single biggest change I make to a struggling site is moving the phone number and WhatsApp button into the header, the hero, and a sticky bar on mobile. On a phone, a customer in a hurry should never have to scroll to find you. On the plumbing site, the emergency number sits in a bar that follows the screen the whole way down.

3. Make the next step obvious and small

"Get a free quote" beats "Contact us." "Book a test drive" beats "Enquire." A specific, low-commitment action gets clicked; a vague one gets ignored. And one clear button beats four competing ones — if everything is emphasised, nothing is.

What I stopped doing

Carousels nobody clicks. Stock photos of people shaking hands in offices. Long paragraphs about company values before the visitor knows what the company does. Every one of those adds weight and removes clarity.

None of this is clever. It's just the difference between a site that looks nice and a site that earns its keep.

Process 4 min read

How I take a site from idea to live in under a week

A landing page in three to five days isn't a marketing promise — it's what happens when you remove the parts of the process that exist to justify an agency invoice.

Most website projects don't take months because the building takes months. They take months because of waiting — for a brief, for approval, for content, for a meeting that gets moved twice.

Day one: one conversation, then a written quote

I ask what the business does, who it's for, and what a good result looks like. That's usually twenty minutes. By the end of the day you have a fixed price, a scope and a delivery date in writing. No proposal document, no discovery workshop.

Day two: structure before design

I write the page order and the actual headline copy first — in plain text, no styling. If the words don't work, no amount of design rescues them. This is the step most people skip, and it's the one that decides whether the site converts.

Days three to four: build, with a live link from the start

You get a working URL on day three, not a screenshot. You can open it on your own phone, send it to a colleague, and tell me what's wrong. Changes happen the same day. This is why there's no big reveal at the end — you've been looking at it all along.

Day five: launch and handover

Domain connected, forms tested, images compressed, page titles written. Then a short walkthrough — usually a screen recording — showing you how to change prices, hours or photos yourself. You get the keys, not a maintenance contract.

What slows a project down

Honestly? Content. If you have your text and photos ready, a landing page is genuinely three days. If we're writing the copy together from scratch, add a few days. I'll always tell you which one you're in before we start, so the date means something.

Plain-English guide

Getting your own domain name.

Right now your site lives at a free address. A domain is just the name people type — yourbusiness.com instead of a long link. Here's the whole process, no jargon.

1

Buy the name

Go to a registrar — Namecheap, Cloudflare or GoDaddy all work. Type your business name and see what's free.

A .com costs roughly $10–15 a year. A country ending like .co.nz or .ng costs a bit more. Pay for one year to start — don't let anyone sell you ten.

Tip: if your first choice is taken, add your town or trade — nelsonplumbing.com reads better than a hyphenated version.

2

Point it at your site

Your registrar will have a section called DNS or Manage DNS. That's where you tell the internet where your site lives.

If your site is hosted on Vercel, go to your project → Settings → Domains → add your new domain. Vercel then shows you exactly two records to copy into your registrar's DNS page — usually an A record and a CNAME record. Copy them across, save, and wait.

It can take a few hours for the change to spread — sometimes up to a day. That's normal. Nothing is broken; it's just propagating.

3

Turn on the padlock

You want https:// and a padlock in the address bar. On Vercel this is one click — SSL is issued automatically once the domain is connected. On other hosts, look for "Free SSL" or "Let's Encrypt" and enable it.

Without it, browsers show "Not secure" next to your name, which quietly costs you trust before a visitor reads a single word.

4

Set up a matching email

hello@yourbusiness.com looks far more credible than a Gmail address on an invoice. Two easy routes:

Cheapest: Cloudflare Email Routing — free. It forwards hello@yourbusiness.com straight to your existing Gmail, and you reply from Gmail. Takes about ten minutes to set up.

Full mailbox: Google Workspace or Zoho Mail, roughly $6/month or free respectively. You get a real inbox you can send from as your business.

Connecting a domain is included when I build your site — you buy the name in your own account (so you always own it), and I'll do the DNS, the padlock and the email forwarding. You never have to look at a settings page.
